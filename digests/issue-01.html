Three events, traced

The birthright citizenship ruling — 5 framings, 15 distinct carriers. Common ground first: every framing accepts that the Court upheld birthright citizenship, that the 14th Amendment and Wong Kim Ark were central, and that an executive order challenging it was rejected. The contest is over what the ruling means: Constitutional Vindication · Executive Overreach · National Sovereignty / Immigration Abuse · Human Cost / Immigrant Families · Originalism Under Scrutiny. All five framings are traced upstream (see the featured trace below).

Trump's $1 billion crypto disclosure — 8 framings, 33 distinct carriers, and the widest framing spread of the week: from Corruption & Conflict of Interest and Retail Investor Harm through Presidential Business Success / American Crypto Leadership (3 carriers) to an intra-right institutional critique ("legal but reckless") — plus a market-structure framing arguing the token's design, not any person, is the story. The specific figures inside each framing ($1.4B, a 97% token decline, retail losses) are the framings' own claims, carried by their sources — click through for who says what.

The transgender sports ruling — 8 framings, 28 carriers, from Competitive Fairness to Civil Rights Violation to Scientific Uncertainty and Political Weaponization. A scope note, in the spirit of showing our work: this analysis partly anchored on the case's longer procedural history, so its event summary reflects an earlier posture of the case than this week's ruling. The framings describe the current discourse; the header sentence is imprecise. We're publishing it with this caveat rather than silently regenerating it.

Featured trace: where a framing comes from

The "birth tourism loophole" framing of this week's birthright ruling is not new — Tributary's trace finds the phrase-family and its idea running back to August 2010, in the "anchor baby" debate over repealing the 14th Amendment's citizenship clause, with 18 dated attestations across 16 years connecting talk-radio, think-tank reports, legislative proposals, and this week's coverage. Open the trace — expand the National Sovereignty framing and click "Trace this framing's origin."

One more, for the connoisseurs: the "originalism is inconsistent" framing — used this week against the 6–3 split — traces to Paul Brest's 1980 law-review article, the paper that coined the word "originalism." A 46-year-old critique, redeployed within hours of a ruling.

What we can't tell you yet

Two things this instrument measures are deliberately not in this issue, because they haven't passed our own published audits:

  • Omission claims ("outlet X never covered story Y"). We ran our audit this week: 10 sampled claims, manually checked against each outlet's own site. Three failed — two because outlets covered the story only as video (which news-sitemaps don't list), one because a BBC article never appeared in its news-sitemap at all. 5/10 held; our bar is 9/10. Until absence claims survive that audit, we don't publish them. The full log is public.
  • Outlet × framing matrices (which framings each outlet's headlines express). The AI labels haven't yet matched human judgment reliably enough to publish (two failed audit rounds, also logged publicly).

A provenance tool should show you its own error rate. Ours: every audit, pass or fail, is in the roadmap and corrections log.
